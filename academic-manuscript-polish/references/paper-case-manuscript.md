# Numbered items and contact lines in two versions of six service cards

**Complete fictional teaching manuscript.** Every document and reading note below was created for PAPER QUEST. This is a worked example of a descriptive document-comparison paper, not a report of real research or a manuscript for submission. The [evidence packet](paper-case-materials.md) supplies all underlying text. The [walkthrough](paper-case-walkthrough.md) explains how each section was developed.

## Abstract

Document revisions can change the grouping of instructions and the information displayed alongside them. In this fictional teaching exercise, we compared the earlier and later versions of six service cards supplied in a closed evidence packet. We counted top-level numbered items and recorded whether each document contained an explicit contact line. The total number of numbered items decreased from 30 to 21, corresponding to means of 5.0 and 3.5 items per card and a 30% reduction relative to the earlier total. All six cards had fewer numbered items in the later version. Contact lines were present on two earlier cards and five later cards. Inspection of the text showed that some later items combined actions previously listed separately. These observations describe the supplied documents; no reader performance or service outcomes were measured. The comparison therefore supports a description of changed document organization, with the usefulness of either version remaining untested.

**Keywords:** document comparison; service instructions; numbered items; contact information; teaching example

## 1. Introduction

Service instructions present actions that readers are expected to follow, but their visible organization and the performance of readers are separate objects of observation. A numbered list provides one way to group those actions. A count of its items can describe the document even when there are no observations of people using it. Teaching Note L1 makes this distinction explicit: several actions can occur within a single numbered item, so the number of items alone cannot establish completion time or comprehension [L1].

Other features require their own definitions. Teaching Note L2 treats an explicit contact line as a recordable document feature and distinguishes its presence from the availability or use of assistance [L2]. Taken together, the two notes support reporting instruction grouping and contact information separately. They provide the conceptual scope of this exercise, not evidence that either feature improves reader outcomes or a review of a real research field.

The supplied packet contains earlier and later versions of six fictional service cards. We ask how the number of numbered items and the presence of a contact line differ between these versions. The purpose is to describe changes within this closed collection, using counts that can be checked against the complete texts. We do not estimate effects on readers or make claims about service documents outside the packet.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Materials and comparison unit

The corpus comprised twelve documents arranged as six matched pairs, labelled C01 to C06. Each pair concerned the same service: reserving a room, renewing a loan, reporting a missing item, requesting a scan, booking a consultation, or returning a kit. The earlier and later labels indicate sequence only; dates were not provided. All six pairs supplied for the exercise were included. There was no sampling from a larger document collection, random assignment, or reader study.

### 2.2 Definitions and recording

For each document, we counted top-level numbered items. A numbered item counted once even if it contained several verbs or described several actions. Titles and contact lines were excluded from this count. We recorded contact-line presence as 1 when a separate line began with `Contact:` and as 0 otherwise. The supplied addresses were fictional; their operation and the availability of assistance were not assessed.

The texts were counted manually using these rules. The exercise contains no independent second-coder assessment or coding-reliability estimate. The complete texts and counting rules are retained in the evidence packet, allowing each recorded value to be checked directly.

### 2.3 Descriptive summaries

We reported counts for each matched pair and summed the numbered items across the six documents in each version. Means were calculated by dividing each total by six. The relative reduction was calculated as the earlier total minus the later total, divided by the earlier total. Contact-line presence was summed separately for each version. These summaries describe the supplied collection; no inferential significance tests were performed.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Numbered items

The total number of numbered items decreased from 30 in the earlier documents to 21 in the later documents (Table 1). The mean decreased from 5.0 to 3.5 items per card. The difference of nine items represents a 30% reduction relative to the earlier total. Every pair contained fewer numbered items in its later version, with reductions ranging from one to three items. C06 showed the largest change, from six items to three.

The final column reports later minus earlier numbered items. The row unit is one paired card; counts are not participant observations.

| C04 | 6 | 4 | 0 | 1 | -2 |

### 3.2 Contact lines and textual grouping

Contact lines appeared on two of the six earlier cards and five of the six later cards. C01, C04, and C06 gained a contact line; C02 and C05 retained one, while C03 had none in either version. This is an increase of three cards, or 50 percentage points in the share containing a contact line.

Text inspection also identified instances of combined grouping. In C01, choosing a room and choosing a time slot appeared as separate earlier items and as one later item. In C06, taking the case to the desk and requesting a receipt likewise moved into one numbered item. These examples show that fewer numbered items can coexist with retention of the named actions.

## 4. Discussion

Within the supplied collection, the later versions consistently contained fewer numbered items, while contact lines appeared on more cards. The first observation answers the question about list organization, and the second describes a distinct document feature. Keeping both observations separate avoids treating a change in one feature as evidence about the other.

The examples from C01 and C06 support the supplied interpretation that some changes reflect how actions are grouped on the page. This accords with L1's distinction between numbered items and underlying actions [L1]. The counting rule permits several actions within one item, so a reduction in the count cannot by itself establish a reduction in what readers must do. No completion-time or comprehension measurements are available to extend the comparison to reader performance.

Similarly, the increased presence of contact lines describes information in the documents. Following the distinction in L2, this does not show that assistance would be available or that readers would use it [L2]. The fictional addresses were not tested, and no service outcomes were recorded.

The scope of the comparison is limited to six supplied pairs. Their inclusion was determined by the teaching packet, with no basis for estimating how representative they are of other documents. The version labels provide no dates, so they do not support a broader time trend. Counting was performed under explicit rules, but no independent coding-reliability assessment is available. These boundaries leave the directly checkable document counts intact while limiting claims about their interpretation and generality.

## 5. Conclusion

Across six fictional paired service cards, later versions contained fewer numbered items and more frequently included an explicit contact line. Textual examples show that some separate actions were grouped under a single later number. The findings describe these documents and leave reader performance and service quality unmeasured.
